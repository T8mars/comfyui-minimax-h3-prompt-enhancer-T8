# Situational drama: a small task and its expectation

Organize the current situation around a small goal, an appropriate attempt and its perceivable consequence. Interest grows from the actual predicament and character response, not from inserting unrelated jokes. The name is the user's local product choice, not a translation of all sitcom theory. This method can serve warm comedy, understated irony, earnest cooperation or an unresolved expectation. Follow the requested tone; selection alone does not authorize humor, mistakes, accidents, new plot or spoken lines.

First identify the given practical goal, existing people, available objects and limits. Preserve competence, dignity and the specified relationships. Do not turn a capable person into a fool, an injured person into a punchline, or a child into a target of collective humiliation to force a gag. In serious or solemn requests, keep the situation serious. A successful cooperative task does not need failure before success. A pure product or mechanical request must not gain human voices, facial expression, distress or an antagonist.

Look for an expectation or constraint already supplied, or specifically permitted by a request to invent this scene. Do not fabricate a danger, deadline, secret, third party or extra prop to create stakes. Distinguish separate permissions: asking for two short lines is not permission to add an accident; asking for a gentle mishap is not permission for injury; a request for a new scene does not cancel a locked outcome. A literal misunderstanding must follow words the audience can hear; do not invent a caption or explanatory narrator to establish it.

Choose one mechanism appropriate to the scene rather than stacking a formula. Useful possibilities include two well-intended attempts that do not initially coordinate, an expectation met in a different allowed way, a supplied phrase taken literally, or a previously established object gaining a new practical use. Some scenes contain none of these: complete their given action faithfully without inventing a comic predicament. Treat setup and payoff as diagnostic questions, not mandatory event counts. Do not force a third repetition, a biggest possible disaster, a three-act arc or a surprise reversal at the end.

Show enough of the allowed setup for the response to be understandable. Keep the audience's and each character's information distinct. Do not label an unexplained event a payoff: its basis must be established in this clip or supplied as prior context. A short excerpt can begin after setup, contain only a response, or end before resolution if requested. Never rewrite an intentionally unfinished wait into a solved problem. When the user asks for a self-contained miniature, prefer one clear task and one modest consequence that can actually fit, not multiple subplots compressed into seconds.

Develop through the characters' own ways of acting. Use a small number of concrete physical attempts or authorized lines, with an intelligible reception and adjustment where the task permits it. Existing tasks, gestures and objects should do double duty when useful, rather than add extra scenery. Track who holds each end of an object, its orientation, clearance and movement through space; make cooperation feasible instead of teleporting, exchanging hands invisibly or passing solid furniture through a door. Preserve fixed waits, positions, action order and contact constraints.

Dialogue is optional. Preserve supplied words and language; create missing lines only when the actual user instruction expressly asks for them under the shared authoring contract. Keep behavior and delivery outside the words. Do not explain the incongruity after showing it, append a second punchline to an emotional moment, or add canned laughter, a cheering audience, comic music, exaggerated sound effects, fourth-wall glances or subtitles without permission. Humor should remain compatible with the requested people and world; warm completion need not become embarrassment or punishment.

Scale to the requested duration and shot policy. A few seconds may support one response to an already established expectation; a longer miniature can support a compact setup, attempt and consequence, but there is no fixed seconds-per-beat or laugh quota. Speaking and moving compete for time. Keep a fixed camera or continuous take; attention changes need not become cuts. Relay events are not sitcom acts. Leave the requested final possession, relation, velocity, door state or unresolved task intact. Trim redundant explanations and late extra gags. Output only the selected H3 or Seedance native final form, never a sitcom script, setup/payoff table, comedy score or imported protocol.
